# GRRM Writing Principles

Core principles for maintaining George R.R. Martin's narrative style.

---

## Principle 1: No Pure Heroes or Villains

### The Rule
Every character is the hero of their own story. No one is purely good or evil.

### Application

**For Protagonist**:
- He has good impulses but can be manipulated
- His need to belong can lead to bad choices
- He may do terrible things for understandable reasons
- He is not automatically right

**For Antagonists**:
- Marcus hunts him but believes he's saving the world
- Gritha seeks revenge but her people were genuinely wronged
- Elara does wrong things for arguably right reasons
- Even vampires weren't purely evil—they were arrogant and cruel, but also created civilization

### What to Avoid
- Character who is always right
- Character who is always wrong
- Villain who is evil for evil's sake
- Hero who never makes mistakes

---

## Principle 2: Actions Have Consequences

### The Rule
Every choice matters. No decision exists without cost or ripple effect.

### Application

**Immediate**:
- Kill someone → their allies respond
- Trust wrong person → betrayal
- Show mercy → may return to help or harm
- Steal artifact → faction responds

**Long-term**:
- Political choices reshape landscape
- Revealed secrets cannot be unrevealed
- Dead characters stay dead
- Broken trust is hard to repair

### What to Avoid
- Consequence-free decisions
- Reset button after major events
- Characters who escape earned fate
- Actions that don't ripple outward

---

## Principle 3: Anyone Can Die

### The Rule
No character has plot armor. Death can come for anyone if the story demands.

### Application

**Major Characters**:
- Any POV character can die
- Death must be earned by story logic
- Death should not be shock value
- Survival is never guaranteed

**When to Kill**:
- Consequences of their choices catch up
- Story is better served
- Theme is expressed
- Reader expectations subverted meaningfully

### What to Avoid
- Killing for shock alone
- Protecting favorites from earned fate
- Bringing back the dead
- Near-death without real risk

---

## Principle 4: Deep POV Technique

### The Rule
Write from inside the character's head. Their biases color everything.

### Application

**Perception**:
- Describe only what POV character notices
- Filter through their understanding
- Their judgments of others are unreliable
- They can be wrong about everything

**Knowledge**:
- Character knows only what they know
- Cannot reveal information character doesn't have
- Reader learns when character learns
- Dramatic irony when reader knows more

### What to Avoid
- Camera-eye neutral descriptions
- Character knowing things they shouldn't
- Author voice intruding
- Breaking POV to show other scenes

---

## Principle 5: Historical Ambiguity

### The Rule
No single true version of history. Multiple interpretations coexist.

### Application

**Conflicting Accounts**:
- Collapse has six different explanations
- Each race remembers events differently
- Even protagonist's memories are unreliable
- Scholars disagree with each other

**Using Ambiguity**:
- Characters argue about the past
- New evidence challenges beliefs
- Reader must judge for themselves
- Some things stay unknown

### What to Avoid
- Definitive "what really happened" without ambiguity
- One race/faction being completely right
- Resolving all mysteries
- Author declaring correct interpretation

---

## Principle 6: Political Complexity

### The Rule
No simple solutions. Competing legitimate interests create impossible choices.

### Application

**Factions**:
- Each faction has valid concerns
- Alliances are conditional
- Betrayal is always possible
- No perfect allies

**Decisions**:
- Every choice has trade-offs
- Supporting one faction hurts another
- Neutral is not always an option
- Good intentions don't guarantee good results

### What to Avoid
- Clear good guys vs bad guys
- Easy solutions to political problems
- Factions that are entirely right/wrong
- Politics that resolve neatly

---

## Principle 7: Sensory Detail

### The Rule
Make the world feel lived-in through specific sensory details.

### Application

**What to Include**:
- Food (what people eat, how it tastes)
- Smells (cities, forests, blood)
- Textures (armor, fabric, skin)
- Sounds (battles, silence, speech patterns)
- Weather (how it affects everything)

**How to Use**:
- Ground scenes in physical reality
- Use details that reveal character
- Make setting feel real
- Connect senses to emotion

### What to Avoid
- Generic descriptions
- Info dumps about world
- Details that don't serve scene
- Forgetting the body

---

## Principle 8: Subtext in Dialogue

### The Rule
Characters rarely say exactly what they mean. Subtext carries the real message.

### Application

**Techniques**:
- What's not said matters
- Power dynamics in every conversation
- Lies and evasions
- Saying one thing, meaning another

**Reveals Character**:
- How they avoid topics
- What they emphasize
- Who they're trying to impress
- What they're hiding

### What to Avoid
- Characters explaining themselves fully
- "As you know, Bob" exposition
- On-the-nose emotional statements
- Dialogue that only conveys information

---

## Principle 9: Earned Moments

### The Rule
No deus ex machina. Payoffs must be set up; seeds must be planted early.

### Application

**Setup and Payoff**:
- Chekhov's gun (if shown, will be used)
- Foreshadowing that's visible in hindsight
- Character skills established before needed
- Solutions come from established elements

**Reader Response**:
- "Of course!" not "What?"
- Satisfaction of pieces fitting
- Surprise that makes sense
- Inevitability in retrospect

### What to Avoid
- Convenient new abilities
- Lucky coincidences
- Problems solved by things not established
- Twists that contradict earlier story

---

## Principle 10: Moral Complexity

### The Rule
Resist easy moral judgments. Explore the gray areas.

### Application

**Questions to Ask**:
- Is this character's choice defensible from their view?
- What would I do in their position?
- Are there legitimate arguments for the "wrong" side?
- Does punishment fit crime?

**In Practice**:
- Protagonist can do bad things
- Antagonists can have valid points
- Good people can make terrible choices
- Bad people can do good things

### What to Avoid
- Clear moral lessons
- Villains who know they're villains
- Heroes who are morally pure
- Story that preaches

---

## Additional Principles

### Show Don't Tell
- Reveal through action and dialogue
- Avoid internal exposition
- Let reader draw conclusions
- Trust the reader

### Pacing Variation
- Action and character moments balance
- Quiet before storm
- Multiple storylines create rhythm
- Don't rush to climaxes

### World as Character
- Setting has personality
- Politics feel organic
- History shapes present
- Magic has rules and costs

### Subverted Expectations
- But not arbitrarily
- Twists must fit
- Reader's assumptions challenged
- Tropes used consciously

---

## Common Pitfalls

### ❌ Over-Explaining
- Trust reader to understand
- Don't explain jokes
- Don't explain character motivations
- Don't explain world mechanics (unless character would)

### ❌ Protecting Favorites
- Characters you love can die
- Characters you love can be wrong
- Characters you love can be villains
- Story trumps attachment

### ❌ Anachronism
- No modern language
- No modern attitudes (presented as normal)
- Technology appropriate to era
- Characters are of their time

### ❌ Rushed Consequences
- Let events breathe
- Don't skip to resolution
- Show the cost
- Take time with aftermath

---

## The Core Truth

GRRM's style works because it treats characters as real people in a real world. Real people are complex. Real choices are hard. Real consequences are inescapable. Real history is debatable. Real power corrupts.

Write the world as if it doesn't care about the protagonist. Write characters as if they don't know they're in a story. Write choices as if they matter forever.
